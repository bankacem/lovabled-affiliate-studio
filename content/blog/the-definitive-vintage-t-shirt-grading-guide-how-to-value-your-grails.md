<p>You see two copies of the same 1994 band shirt — one priced at a fraction of the other. The difference is almost always grading. In vintage apparel, grading is not just about how "clean" a shirt looks; it is an assessment of structural integrity, print condition, and history. Unlike comics or coins, "perfect" is not always the most valuable — but a consistent, honest grading system is what separates trusted sellers from everyone else. This guide gives you the scale, the inspection routine, and the fatal flaws to watch for.</p>

<section id="why-grading">
<h2>Why Grading Decides Value</h2>
<p>Condition is the single biggest factor separating two "identical" vintage shirts. Two copies of the same design — one deadstock, one thrashed — can be worth dramatically different amounts. What makes vintage unusual is that wear is not always a penalty: in 90s grunge and early-80s punk, heavy wear <em>is</em> the desired aesthetic, and thrashed examples can outperform pristine ones. The market for each category decides which condition it wants. Your job as a grader is to describe condition precisely and completely so buyers know exactly what they are getting.</p>

<section id="the-scale">
<h2>The Grading Scale Collectors Use</h2>
<p>Vintage clothing has no single grading authority, but the resale community has converged on a descriptive hierarchy. Learn it and use it consistently:</p>
<h3>Deadstock / Mint</h3>
<p>Never worn, never washed. Tag and print pristine. True deadstock — a shirt that sat in a warehouse for thirty years — is the rarest condition. Be honest with yourself: a shirt washed even once is not deadstock.</p>
<h3>Excellent</h3>
<p>Worn and washed, but with no serious issues: no stains, holes, rips, or seam problems. Minor fade or a faint mark is acceptable. Most of the best wearable grails live here.</p>
<h3>Great</h3>
<p>One serious issue, or several minor ones — a tiny hole, a small underarm seam separation, a few faint marks. Still very wearable; this is where much of the actual buying and selling happens.</p>
<h3>Good</h3>
<p>Noticeably worn: more significant marks, a few small holes, heavier fade. The shirt has character and is priced accordingly.</p>
<h3>Fair / Worn</h3>
<p>Years of wear and washing. Multiple marks, several small holes, thinned fabric — but construction is still stable. Genuinely sought after for the authentic vintage look in grunge and punk niches.</p>
<h3>Poor to Distressed</h3>
<p>Gaping holes, major seam issues, heavy staining. At this end you are buying the graphic and the vibe, not a wearable garment. Distressed shirts still sell, but only to buyers who want exactly that look.</p>
<h3>The "See-Through" Modifier</h3>
<p>Fabric worn so thin you can faintly see through it. This modifier can apply anywhere from Excellent to Distressed. Some collectors love it — it proves decades of genuine wear. Disclose it either way.</p>
</section>

<section id="inspection">
<h2>The Inspection Checklist</h2>
<p>Inspect top to bottom, every time:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>The collar:</strong> check for "bacon neck" — the waviness that appears when the ribbing's elastic fails. A stretched collar drops a shirt's grade noticeably.</li>
<li><strong>The stitching:</strong> look at the sleeve and bottom hems. Single-stitch generally indicates pre-1994 production. Check for loose threads or unraveling at the hems.</li>
<li><strong>The pit test:</strong> turn the shirt inside out and check the armpits for yellowing or stiffness. Often permanent, and it devalues the piece.</li>
<li><strong>The side seams:</strong> is the shirt tubular (one piece of fabric) or does it have side seams? Tubular construction is common on true vintage and avoids the twisting seen in cheaper modern garments.</li>
<li><strong>The print:</strong> examine cracking, flaking, and fading. Fine, organic cracking is normal aging; large flakes lifting off the fabric are damage.</li>
<li><strong>The dry-rot tug test:</strong> grab an inconspicuous area of fabric and give it a firm tug. If it rips like tissue paper with a distinct zipper sound, the shirt has dry rot — a chemical breakdown of black-dyed cotton where sulfur in the dye eats the fibers. Dry rot cannot be fixed. The shirt is a display piece, not a wearable one.</li>
</ul>
</section>

<section id="good-vs-bad">
<h2>Good Wear vs. Bad Wear</h2>
<p>Not all damage is equal. Learn the distinction:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Good wear:</strong> even fading, fine print cracking, soft thinning, sun-fade on the shoulders. These prove age and add character.</li>
<li><strong>Bad wear:</strong> dry rot, large holes at stress points, pit staining, bacon neck, unraveling seams. These compromise the garment.</li>
</ul>
<p>The tricky middle ground is "thrashed" — heavy wear that the market actively wants in certain niches. A thrashed grunge tee is not a damaged tee; it is a tee in the condition its buyers prefer. Grade it accurately as Fair/Worn or Distressed, describe the wear precisely, and let the market decide.</p>
</section>

<section id="grading-for-sale">
<h2>Grading a Shirt You Are Selling</h2>
<p>The most successful vintage sellers share one habit: brutal honesty. The routine:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Photograph every flaw.</strong> If there is a hole the size of a grain of rice, find it, photograph it in good light, and mention it.</li>
<li><strong>Grade down, not up.</strong> When torn between two grades, choose the lower one. Buyers who receive a shirt better than described become repeat customers; buyers who receive worse leave returns.</li>
<li><strong>Measure, don't eyeball.</strong> Provide pit-to-pit and length measurements. A vintage Large is often a modern Medium, and sizing disputes are the most common return trigger.</li>
<li><strong>Disclose the tag situation.</strong> Missing tag? Say so, and explain how you dated the shirt (stitching, print, fabric).</li>
<li><strong>Describe odor honestly.</strong> Storage smell that washes out is fine to note; smoke or mildew that does not is a material defect.</li>
</ol>
<p>Transparency builds the reputation that makes your "Mint" claims believable when you actually have one.</p>
</section>

<section id="buying-graded">
<h2>Buying Graded Pieces</h2>
<p>When buying, treat grades as a starting point, not a guarantee:</p>
<ul>
<li>Ask for photos of the collar, pits, hems, and tag — the four spots sellers most often skip.</li>
<li>Ask how the shirt was stored. Warehouse finds and attic finds age very differently.</li>
<li>Be extra cautious with "deadstock" claims on high-value pieces — a mint 30-year-old shirt is rare enough that counterfeiters fake the condition too.</li>
<li>Check the seller's other listings. Consistent, detailed grading across listings is a trust signal; vague one-word grades are not.</li>
<li>Watch for "grade inflation" language: "minty," "near deadstock," and "excellent for its age" are not grades. Ask the seller to map their description to the scale — deadstock, excellent, great, good, fair, or distressed — and to name the specific flaws.</li>
<li>For high-value pieces, ask for a short video of the tug test area or the collar stretch. A seller confident in their grade will provide it; one who deflects is telling you something.</li>
</ul>
</section>

<section id="grading-disputes">
<h2>Common Grading Disputes and How to Avoid Them</h2>
<p>Most vintage returns and disputes come down to grading disagreements. The usual flashpoints:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Fade vs. stain:</strong> Photograph the shirt in natural daylight. What looks like "even fade" under warm indoor light can read as discoloration in daylight — and buyers will photograph it in daylight when disputing.</li>
<li><strong>"Small hole" size creep:</strong> A pinhole and a dime-sized hole are different grades. Put a coin next to the flaw in the photo so the scale is unambiguous.</li>
<li><strong>Washed vs. unwashed claims:</strong> Never claim deadstock unless you are certain. A shirt that smells of detergent has been washed, full stop.</li>
<li><strong>Tag condition:</strong> A crispy intact tag supports a higher grade; a frayed or half-missing tag should be disclosed even when the body of the shirt is excellent.</li>
</ul>
<p>The underlying principle: grade for the pickiest buyer you can imagine, and you will rarely have a dispute with a real one.</p>

<section class="faq" itemscope itemtype="https://schema.org/FAQPage">
<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<div itemscope itemprop="mainEntity" itemtype="https://schema.org/Question">
<h3 itemprop="name">Is a thrashed shirt worth less than a mint one?</h3>
<div itemscope itemprop="acceptedAnswer" itemtype="https://schema.org/Answer">
<p itemprop="text">Not necessarily. In 90s grunge and early-80s punk, thrashed shirts with heavy wear are often the most coveted. Each niche's market decides which condition it values most.</p>
</div>
</div>
<div itemscope itemprop="mainEntity" itemtype="https://schema.org/Question">
<h3 itemprop="name">Does washing a vintage shirt lower its grade?</h3>
<div itemscope itemprop="acceptedAnswer" itemtype="https://schema.org/Answer">
<p itemprop="text">Technically, yes — once washed, a shirt is no longer deadstock. But a careful hand-wash can improve a Fair shirt's grade by removing decades of storage odor or light dust.</p>
</div>
</div>
<div itemscope itemprop="mainEntity" itemtype="https://schema.org/Question">
<h3 itemprop="name">What is sun-fade and does it add value?</h3>
<div itemscope itemprop="acceptedAnswer" itemtype="https://schema.org/Answer">
<p itemprop="text">Natural bleaching from UV exposure, often on the shoulders. Many collectors value it because it is impossible to replicate naturally and gives the shirt a unique patina.</p>
</div>
</div>
<div itemscope itemprop="mainEntity" itemtype="https://schema.org/Question">
<h3 itemprop="name">Can dry rot be fixed?</h3>
<div itemscope itemprop="acceptedAnswer" itemtype="https://schema.org/Answer">
<p itemprop="text">No. Dry rot is a structural failure of cotton fibers at the molecular level. No treatment restores the fabric's strength once rotting has begun.</p>
</div>
</div>
<div itemscope itemprop="mainEntity" itemtype="https://schema.org/Question">
<h3 itemprop="name">How do I grade a shirt with a missing tag?</h3>
<div itemscope itemprop="acceptedAnswer" itemtype="https://schema.org/Answer">
<p itemprop="text">Grade the condition normally, but note the missing tag explicitly and date the shirt via stitching, fabric, and print. Missing tags reduce buyer certainty, so price and describe accordingly.</p>
</div>
